# Spindle · validation kit

A single document containing the three things you need over the next two weeks: outreach to book the calls, the interview to run on the calls, and a small framework for reading what you hear.

Goal of the kit: **eight 25-minute calls with senior fashion product-team people who could become founding customers.** That's it. Not a survey. Not a focus group. Eight specific humans with their hands on the work, telling you in their own words whether they'd pay.

---

## Part 1 — The outreach

You have 15 years of fashion network. You know who to ask. The question is *how* to ask without making it weird — because some of these people are friends, some are colleagues from old jobs, some are senior people you'd like to keep as references later.

The right framing: **you're not selling anything yet. You're asking for a critique.** People will say yes to "tear apart my idea for 25 minutes" who would never say yes to "let me pitch you something." Also — you're being transparent that they're early, which is flattering rather than transactional.

Three DM templates for three relationships. Use the right one for each contact.

### Template A — for friends and old colleagues you know well

> Hey [name],
>
> Working on something fashion-ops related and I want to tear it apart with someone who'll actually be honest with me. You're who I thought of.
>
> 25 mins on Zoom — I show you a working prototype, you tell me where it's wrong. No deck, no pitch, no follow-up unless you want one.
>
> Free any time this week or next? Happy to come to you on whatever calendar's easiest.
>
> [your name]

### Template B — for senior contacts you respect but don't know intimately

> Hi [name],
>
> Hope you're well. Quick ask — I'm building a tool for fashion product teams — buyers, merchandisers, sourcing, production. The pain it's solving is the chaos of email, WhatsApp, and tracker.xlsx all trying to be the source of truth for production status. You'd recognise the shape.
>
> I'd really value 25 minutes of your honest reaction to a working prototype. Not a pitch — I genuinely want to know if I'm getting the problem right. Your name has come up several times when I think about who'd see through the bullshit fastest.
>
> Any window over the next two weeks? Happy to work around your calendar entirely.
>
> [your name]

### Template C — for people you barely know (cold-warm)

> Hi [name],
>
> We crossed paths briefly at [event/role/mutual contact] — I've been following your work at [their brand]. I'm building something for fashion ops and would love 25 minutes of brutally honest feedback on a prototype.
>
> The thesis: every PLM tells merchandisers to stop using email and Excel. Nobody stops. So we built something that works *alongside* email and Excel instead of against them — a Gmail/Outlook plugin that reads your supplier emails and builds a live style card for every line. The prototype already runs on real data.
>
> No pitch — I'm in research mode. Any chance you'd take 25 mins next week or the week after?
>
> [your name] · [link to spindle landing page]

### What to do with the responses

- **Yes, this week:** book it. Don't delay. Momentum compounds.
- **Yes but in 3 weeks:** book it. Don't push for sooner. People remember when you're respectful of their calendar.
- **No reply after 7 days:** one follow-up, one line: *"Bumping this up — happy to make it 15 mins if that's easier."* Then stop. Move on.
- **"Sounds interesting, send me a link":** send the landing page. Don't pitch in the DM. The landing page does the pitching.

Aim for **8 confirmed calls in 14 days.** If you hit 8 from your first 12 DMs, your network is warm and you can start building. If you hit 8 from 40 DMs, you have a positioning problem to solve first — go back and tighten the framing.

## Part 2 — The interview

This is the script you run on the call. **Print it.** Have it next to you. Don't improvise — improvising is how founders accidentally pitch instead of listening.

The whole thing takes 22–25 minutes. Time-box it ruthlessly so the person walks away thinking "that was respectful and short" rather than "that was longer than I expected."

### Before the call (60 seconds of prep)

- Pull up the four Spindle artifacts in tabs:
  - `spindle-board.html` — the production board
  - `spindle-style-card.html` — single style detail
  - `spindle-today.html` — the inbox triage view
  - `spindle-landing.html` — the landing page
- Open Loom or Zoom recording. **Get verbal permission to record at the start.** Always.
- Have a Notion doc or a notebook open titled with their name + date.

### Opening — 1 minute

Say exactly this:

> *"Thanks for the time. Quick ground rules: I'm going to ask you about your work for about ten minutes, then show you a prototype for ten, then ask what you'd pay. There's no right answer — you're doing me a favour by telling me where I'm wrong. Mind if I record this for my own notes? It won't go anywhere."*

Wait for the yes. Hit record.

### The seven questions

**Q1 — Walk me through your morning.**

> *"Imagine it's a normal Monday at 9am. You open your laptop. What's the first thing you do, and what's the second?"*

Don't lead. Don't suggest. Sit in silence if they pause. Most merchandisers will say something like *"I open my email and start triaging,"* or *"I open the tracker and look at this week's deliveries."* The shape of the answer tells you whether they live in inbox or in Excel — both are valid customers, but the messaging differs.

**Listen for:** what they open *first*, how long it takes them to feel "on top" of things, the first interruption that derails them.

---

**Q2 — Tell me about the last time you missed something.**

> *"I'm not asking about a screw-up — I'm asking about a moment where you had information somewhere and didn't connect to it in time. The dropped ball. Walk me through that one."*

This is the most important question in the whole script. **Sit through the silence.** Most people will need 5–10 seconds to access a real memory. Don't rescue them.

**Listen for:** what kind of information was it (cost? delivery? approval?). Where did it live (email? WhatsApp? someone's head?). What was the cost (commercial? reputational? personal stress?). How recent was it (last week is gold; "ages ago" is a yellow flag).

If they can't think of one, the pain isn't acute enough — they probably aren't your customer. That's useful data.

---

**Q3 — Show me your tracker.**

> *"Would you mind sharing your screen and walking me through the spreadsheet you actually use to track production? Not the one you'd show your boss — the real one."*

This is the moment that changes everything. **Most people will hesitate.** Reassure: *"I'll close my own screen. I just want to understand how you think about your styles."*

Watch what they do. The columns they have. The colours they use. The notes they've added in the cells. The tabs they have open at the same time. The Slack/WhatsApp/inbox they tab into mid-explanation.

**Listen for:** which columns are clean (recent attention) vs which are stale (decay zones). Where free-text comments live. How they handle dates. Whether they say anything like *"this should be updated but it's not"* or *"this is from before the change."*

---

**Q4 — Where does the data actually live?**

> *"Looking at this row right here — let's say a date changed yesterday. Where would the original message have come from?"*

Pick a row, any row, and ask. Their answer is the use case spec for Spindle.

**Listen for:** how many systems they touch to update one row. How often it's email vs WhatsApp vs phone call vs in-person. Whether they say things like *"I'd have to go look in my Outlook"* — that's the moment you politely note: *"That's a moment Spindle would help with."*

---

### Now show them the prototype — 8–10 minutes

Share screen. Open files in this order, **with no preamble** — let them react first.

**5a. Open `spindle-board.html`.** Say only:

> *"This is everything you'd be tracking — say 247 styles. Each row has a mini timeline that infers where it sits in the production calendar. Tell me what you notice first."*

Then **stop talking.** Sit through the silence. The first thing they zoom in on is your headline.

**5b. Click into a style they pointed at.** (Or default to opening `spindle-style-card.html`.)

> *"This is the page for one style. Built entirely from supplier emails Spindle has been reading over a few weeks. No one entered any of this manually."*

Watch which section they look at longest. The flat sketch? The approval timeline? The conversation digest? Their eye direction is feature priority data.

**5c. Open `spindle-today.html`.** Say:

> *"And this is what they'd see Monday morning. Five things ranked. Most importantly — one of these is from 23 days ago. Spindle re-prioritised it because something changed today."*

**This is the test.** If they react to the 23-day-old re-surfacing, your wedge is real. If they shrug, your wedge is somewhere else.

---

**Q5 — What's wrong with this?**

> *"Where does this fall apart for you? What's missing, what's wrong, what would never work in your team?"*

The answers here are gold. Whatever they say, **don't defend.** Just write it down. Then ask:

> *"Anything else?"*

Then ask again:

> *"What's the second thing?"*

People give you the polite answer first and the honest answer third. Wait for the third.

---

**Q6 — Who in your organisation would actually use this?**

> *"If we deployed this tomorrow at [their brand], who'd open it on day one? And who would fight it?"*

This is your buyer-vs-user-vs-blocker map. The "fight it" question is the most undervalued — it tells you who you'll need to manage in a sale.

**Listen for:** Senior merchandisers vs junior assistants. Production managers. Sourcing directors. Finance ("what about the costing trail"). IT ("what about security"). The CEO ("we're not paying for another tool" or "this is exactly what I've been asking for").

---

**Q7 — What would you pay?**

Read this exactly:

> *"Last question. If this existed and worked the way you just saw — not perfectly, but well enough — what's a reasonable monthly price per merchandiser? Not what's cheap, not what's expensive. What feels fair."*

**Stop talking. Wait.**

The first number they say is the most honest. The second number, after they think about it, is anchored toward their procurement experience.

**Then:**

> *"And if I told you the price was £149 per seat per month — what's your reaction?"*

Watch the face. Surprise (good — you're under their expectation). Polite agreement (medium — anchor too low). Hesitation (anchor about right). "That's a lot" (anchor too high — but ask why, because it might be a budget owner issue, not a value issue).

---

### Closing — 1 minute

> *"Last question — would you be willing to be one of the first 8 founding customers? Free for the first 90 days, founder pricing locked for life after that?"*

**This is the real test.** Anyone who says yes here, write down. Anyone who says "send me more info" is a maybe — they liked it but aren't ready. Anyone who says "let me think about it" is a no.

Then:

> *"Thank you. Two follow-ups — first, is there anyone else in your network you think I should talk to? Second, if I send you weekly updates as we build, are you happy to keep watching?"*

Both yes-answers are gold. Referrals compound. Watchers become customers later.

## Part 3 — How to read what you hear

After every call, fill out this five-line summary in your notes within 10 minutes of hanging up. **Not later.** Memory degrades fast.

```
Name & role:
Acute pain (Q2): yes / no / sort-of
Wedge reaction (23-day re-surface): obvious / muted / didn't notice
Price floor & ceiling (Q7): £___ to £___
Founding customer? (closing): yes / maybe / no
```

After eight calls, look at the table. Three patterns matter:

### Pattern 1 — How many had acute pain?

If 6+/8 had a recent, specific dropped-ball story: **the pain is real.** You can build.
If 3–5/8: the pain is real for a subset. Refine your ICP. Maybe it's only at brands above a certain size, or only in certain product categories.
If 0–2/8: the pain you imagine isn't the pain they feel. **Do not start building.** Re-do the script with a different angle.

### Pattern 2 — How many lit up at the 23-day re-surface?

This is your wedge test. If 5+/8 had a visible reaction (lean forward, *"oh that's the bit I need,"* immediate story about a time this happened to them): **the wedge is the time-aware re-prioritisation.** Lead with it everywhere.
If most of them lit up at the **style card** instead: your wedge is the *single-page memory of every style*. Different positioning.
If most lit up at the **production board**: your wedge is *visual scannability across the whole line*. Different again.

All three are valid wedges. Knowing which one *they* see first determines your homepage headline.

### Pattern 3 — How many said yes to founding customer?

If 4+/8 said yes: **you have eight ready-to-go pilots from twelve DMs.** Start building immediately. You have product-market evidence.
If 1–3/8: you have a real opportunity but the framing isn't quite right. Iterate the script and do another five calls.
If 0/8 said yes: stop. Whatever you think the product is, that isn't it. Rerun Q2 across a wider sample first.

## What to do *after* the eight calls

Two things, in order, before any code:

**1. Pick three brands** that said yes to "founding customer." Email them this:

> *"Thanks again for the call. As I mentioned, you'd be one of the first 8 brands on Spindle. To make sure I build the right thing, would you be willing to send me 30 anonymised supplier emails / WhatsApp screenshots from your last 60 days? I'll use them to test the extraction quality before any code is built. Ten minutes of forwarding for you, weeks of saved work for me."*

If 2+ say yes, you have a real test set.

**2. Run those 90 messages through Claude with a fashion-tuned prompt** and measure: (a) does Spindle correctly identify the style? (b) does it correctly classify what kind of message it is — costing, lab dip, fit comments, cancellation, etc.? (c) when you show the merchant the extraction, do they say "that's right" 85%+ of the time?

**If yes — build.** You have validated demand and validated technical feasibility. The 14-week clock starts.

**If no — don't build yet.** Find the gap between what Claude extracts and what the merchandiser expected. The gap is the schema you need to write by hand. That's the work, and it's worth doing in a notebook for two weeks before any line of production code.

## A note on what *not* to do

- **Don't show the prototype before Q1–Q4.** Their answers without context are the most valuable. Once they've seen the prototype, they answer through its frame.
- **Don't argue with their critique.** Even when they're "wrong," they're representative of how a hundred merchants will react. The argument you'd win on a call is the messaging you'd lose on the landing page.
- **Don't overpromise.** Founding customers tolerate bugs. They don't tolerate lies. If something isn't built yet, say *"that's a v2"* — not *"yeah we have that."*
- **Don't take notes on the call.** Be present. Record. Take notes after.
- **Don't ask "would you pay £149?"** as a yes/no. Ask the open-ended price question first, then anchor.

## The two weeks, day by day

| Day | Action |
|---|---|
| Day 1–2 | Send 12–15 DMs using templates above |
| Day 3–10 | Run 8 calls as they land |
| Day 4 | After call 2, refine the script if anything surprised you |
| Day 8 | After call 5, eyeball the pattern across calls 1–5 |
| Day 11 | After call 8, write the five-line summary table |
| Day 12 | Decide: pattern is strong → ask for test data · pattern is weak → re-run |
| Day 13–14 | Email 3 brands for test data, OR rewrite script and send next round |

Keep a single Notion doc called *"Spindle calls · Q1 2026"* with all 8 transcripts and your post-call notes. You will reference it for two years.

---

That's it. Eight calls, seven questions, three artifacts, three templates. The most important hour of work in the entire Spindle project is sitting in silence after Q2 of the first call. **Don't talk during that silence.** Their answer there is what you're going to build.

Good luck.
